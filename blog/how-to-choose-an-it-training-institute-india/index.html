How to check an IT training institute before you pay

Written by a training provider, which you should factor in. Every check here can be run on us, and two of them we currently fail.

By Ms Puja, CEO and Organizer · 29 August 2026 · 9 min read

Indian IT training is largely unregulated, and the gap between the best and worst providers is enormous. We run training ourselves, so read this with that in mind — and then run every check on us too. We have noted where we currently fall short.

1. Is the price published?

Open the course page. If the fee is not on it, that is the first and most reliable signal.

"Contact us for pricing" on a standard training course exists for one reason: so the price can be adjusted to what the individual caller seems able to pay, usually after a conversation designed to establish that. Genuine enterprise training is quoted per batch and that is legitimate. An individual seat on a scheduled cohort has a price, and there is no honest reason to hide it.

2. Does anyone guarantee you a job?

Walk away. Not "be careful" — walk away.

Nobody can guarantee employment, because the employer makes that decision and they are not party to your agreement. What the guarantee actually is, in every case we have examined, is a refund clause with conditions attached that are nearly impossible to satisfy: attend every session, apply to a set number of companies per week, accept any offer above a threshold, submit evidence in a specified format within a specified window. Miss one condition and the guarantee lapses.

There is a legitimate version of this and it sounds different: placement assistance, resume review, interview practice, introductions where we have them. That is a service. A guarantee is a sales device.

3. Who is actually teaching, and what do they do the rest of the week?

Ask directly: who takes this batch, and what do they work on outside teaching?

The answer you want is a specific person doing the work currently. The answer that should worry you is a shifting "industry expert with 15+ years" that never resolves into a name, or a trainer whose entire recent experience is training. Technology moves; someone who last touched production five years ago will teach you what was true then.

Ask to attend one session before paying. A provider confident in its teaching will agree. One that refuses is telling you something.

4. What happens to your money if it goes wrong?

Get the refund policy in writing before paying anything, and read it for these specifics:

  • How long the refund window is, and from what date it runs — enrolment, or the batch start.
  • How many sessions you can have attended and still qualify.
  • What happens if the institute cancels or postpones the batch. This is the clause that reveals most. A fair policy refunds you in full whenever that happens, because the failure is theirs.
  • Whether the booking amount is refundable or forfeited.
  • How long the refund takes, stated in days.

A policy that only exists verbally is not a policy. If it is not on the website or in an email you can keep, treat it as absent.

5. Are you being pressured?

Countdown timers, "two seats left", a discount that expires this evening, a follow-up call within an hour of your enquiry — these are conversion tactics, and their presence tells you what the organisation optimises for.

Good training has no reason to rush you. If a batch is genuinely full, the honest response is to tell you the next date. Legitimate early-bird pricing exists, but it has a stated date rather than a timer that resets when you reload the page.

6. Are you being asked to pay a certification exam fee?

You book and pay for AWS, Microsoft, Oracle and PeopleCert exams directly with the certifying body or its authorised exam provider. A training institute has no reason to hold your exam money.

Institutes that collect exam fees sometimes never book the exam, sometimes book a cheaper unofficial one, and sometimes simply add a margin. In all three cases you are worse off than paying the vendor yourself. This one check removes a whole category of loss.

7. Do the reviews look like reviews?

Testimonials on a provider's own website are marketing and cannot be verified — including ours, which is why we do not publish any.

Look for reviews you can trace to a person: a LinkedIn profile with a real work history, a post with comments on it, a Google review from an account with other reviews. A page of first-name-and-photo quotes proves nothing at all. And be equally suspicious of the opposite pattern — a brand-new provider with two hundred five-star reviews and no other footprint.

A new institute with no reviews is not automatically a problem. It is a reason to insist harder on everything else in this list.

8. Can you find the organisation outside its own website?

Search the company name, the founder's name, and the name plus words like refund or complaint. Check whether a registered entity exists, whether there is a Google Business Profile, whether the founder has a professional presence you can look at.

Absence of a bad record is not evidence of a good one, but a complete absence of any independent trace — no registration, no named people, no history anywhere — for an organisation asking for tens of thousands of rupees is a legitimate reason to hesitate.

9. What are they explicitly not promising?

This is the check almost nobody runs, and it is the most revealing one.

Read the site for what it refuses to claim. A provider that states plainly that it does not guarantee jobs, that salary figures are market observations rather than commitments, and that certification exams are booked separately, is a provider that has thought about where it could mislead you and decided not to.

A site on which every statement is a promise and nothing is a limitation has been written entirely to convert you.

Run these on us

It would be dishonest to publish this list and exempt ourselves, so: the fee is on every course page; we state explicitly that we do not guarantee jobs or income; the refund terms are published including the clause that we refund in full if we cancel; we do not collect certification exam fees; there are no countdown timers anywhere on this site; and the first class is free so you can judge the teaching before paying.

Where we currently fall short: we are new, so we have no independent reviews for you to check, and we have no Google Business Profile yet. Those are real gaps and we would rather name them than have you discover them. The free first class exists precisely because we know we have not earned trust yet.

In short

  • Published price, written refund policy, named trainer, no job guarantee. Those four filter out most bad providers.
  • Never pay a certification exam fee to a training institute — book directly with the vendor.
  • Urgency tactics tell you what the organisation optimises for.
  • Read what a provider refuses to promise. It is more informative than anything it does promise.

Questions people ask about this

Are job guarantee courses in India legitimate?

The guarantee is not. No training provider controls hiring decisions, so what is being sold is a conditional refund — and the conditions are typically extensive enough that most claimants fail one. Placement assistance is a real service; a guarantee is a sales device.

Is it safe to pay a training institute in advance?

It depends entirely on what you can recover. Pay only where the refund policy is written down, names a window and a process, and covers what happens if the institute cancels. Never pay a large sum before seeing the teaching.

Should I pay my certification exam fee to the training institute?

No. AWS, Microsoft, Oracle and PeopleCert exams are booked and paid directly with the certifying body or its authorised exam provider. There is no legitimate reason for a training company to hold that money.

How do I check if a training institute is genuine?

Published fees, a written refund policy, a named trainer you can ask about, no job guarantee, no exam fee collection, no artificial urgency, and some trace of the organisation existing outside its own website. Missing any one is a question; missing several is an answer.

Is a new training institute with no reviews a red flag?

Not by itself — every provider starts somewhere. It means the other checks have to carry more weight, and it is entirely reasonable to ask for a free or trial session before committing money to anyone without a track record.
